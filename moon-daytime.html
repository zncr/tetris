Why can we see the Moon in the daytime?

The Moon is always there, orbiting Earth once every ~29.5 days. Move the sliders or press play to see when it is above your horizon, lit by the Sun, and bright enough to spot.

The idea in four steps

  1. The Moon is above your horizon about 12 hours every day — just like the Sun — because Earth spins once a day. Those 12 hours are not always at night.
  2. The Sun lights half the Moon. Sunlight keeps shining on the Moon whether or not it is dark where you stand. The lit part is what you see as the phase.
  3. Daytime sky is only bright because of the atmosphere. Air scatters blue light everywhere. Turn off the atmosphere above and the sky goes black, even at noon.
  4. The Moon is brighter than that blue sky as long as a good chunk of it is lit. Its "moonrise" shifts ~50 minutes later each day, so it spends part of the daytime above the horizon on most days: afternoons for a waxing Moon, mornings for a waning one. The exceptions are near full (it rises as the Sun sets) and near new (lit side faces away and it sits next to the Sun).

Simplified model: you are on the equator and the Moon orbits in the same plane as the Sun's path, so everything follows the same east-to-west arc. Real skies are tilted, but the timing story is the same.
